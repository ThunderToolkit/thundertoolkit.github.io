Investor Guide

Turning the tumor's own macrophages against it.

Thunder Biotech is a Provo, Utah company developing MOTO-CAR, a macrophage-based immunotherapy for solid tumors. This guide covers the science, who else is working in the field, where Thunder fits, and the patents behind it.

The Science

CAR-T therapies transformed blood cancers but have struggled in solid tumors, which make up most cancer cases. Macrophages are emerging as the answer to that problem.

What Thunder set out to build: a cancer treatment that is
EffectiveReprograms the tumor's own macrophages to attack it
Less toxicShort-lived CAR expression; macrophages cannot cause graft-versus-host disease
Widely applicableTargets solid tumors, where most cancer cases occur
Off-the-shelfGiven by IV injection; no specialized facilities or cell manufacturing
Up to ~50%

of the immune cells inside some solid tumors are macrophages. They are already there, in large numbers.

No GvHD

Macrophages lack a T-cell receptor, so they cannot cause graft-versus-host disease. Reviews also report lower cytokine-release risk than CAR-T.

3 ways to kill

Macrophages engulf tumor cells, present tumor antigens to T cells, and release signals that recruit the rest of the immune system.

The problem: tumors recruit macrophages to protect them

Inside a solid tumor, many macrophages are switched into an "M2" state that suppresses the immune system and helps the tumor grow. These are called tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Flipping them into the "M1" state turns them into cancer killers.

How MOTO-CAR works

Deliver the instructions

A lipid nanoparticle (LNP) carries genetic instructions for a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) to macrophages, including inside the body ("in vivo"). The nanoparticle is decorated to target tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and is given by IV injection.

Recognize the tumor

The CAR recognizes a marker on cancer cells. Thunder's lead program targets mesothelin, which is expressed in many cancer types.

Flip the switch

When the CAR binds its target, it signals the macrophage to shift toward the cancer-fighting M1 state.

Bring in reinforcements

Activated macrophages attack tumor cells and recruit T cells, which may build immune memory against recurrence.

Why in vivo matters. Most early CAR-macrophage therapies took cells out of the patient, engineered them in a lab, and infused them back. That is slow and expensive, and macrophages are hard to engineer and don't multiply. Programming macrophages inside the body with an LNP avoids cell manufacturing altogether. This is the direction the field has moved, and it is the approach in Thunder's lead program.

How MOTO-CARs remodel the tumor

Once switched on, MOTO-CAR macrophages change the tumor environment so the immune system can attack the cancer effectively, by:

Targeting the tumor

Recognizing a tumor-specific antigen, such as mesothelin.

Killing directly

Engulfing and destroying tumor cells.

Changing the signals

Secreting signals that create an anti-tumor immune environment.

Recruiting help

Drawing other immune cells, like T cells, into the tumor.

Activating T cells

Presenting tumor neoantigens so T cells learn to kill the cancer.

Building memory

Creating durable immune memory that may protect against recurrence.

Short-lived by design. MOTO-CARs are expressed only temporarily. They do their work in the tumor and then fade, which is intended to limit long-term side effects while the immune response they start, including T-cell memory, carries on.

Where the field stands in 2026

  • First generation (ex vivo) stalled. Carisma Therapeutics, the first company to put a CAR-macrophage into human trials, discontinued its clinical programs and began winding down in 2025. Why Carisma failed Carisma vs. Thunder →
  • In vivo is producing the first human signals. In November 2025, CREATE Medicines (formerly Myeloid Therapeutics) reported a confirmed partial response and evidence of CAR-positive myeloid cells inside tumors in a 27-patient Phase 1 study of its in vivo mRNA-LNP therapy.
  • Large pharma is buying in vivo platforms. AstraZeneca, AbbVie, Gilead/Kite, Bristol Myers Squibb and Eli Lilly (twice) acquired in vivo CAR companies between March 2025 and April 2026, in deals worth $350 million to $7 billion. See Deals & Valuations
  • Remaining challenges. Short persistence of engineered macrophages, accumulation in the liver and spleen, and standardizing how strongly cells are polarized.

Why Carisma failed, and what it means for Thunder

Carisma's therapy was safe and did what the science predicted: the engineered macrophages reached tumors, reshaped the tumor environment and expanded cancer-fighting T cells. The company failed on its business model, its target and its funding, not on the macrophage concept.

1. Costly, slow cell manufacturingEach dose was made from the patient's own cells in a lab and infused back (ex vivo). Macrophages don't multiply, so every patient required a full manufacturing run. Carisma replaced CT-0508 with CT-0525, designed in part to cut manufacturing time.
2. Early results were modestIn its 14-patient Phase 1 trial, there were no dose-limiting side effects, but no tumors shrank enough to count as a response. The best result was stable disease in 4 of 9 patients with high HER2.
3. A crowded, unstable targetCarisma said it dropped CT-0525 (Dec 2024) because of the competitive landscape: newly approved HER2 drugs, and tumors losing or reducing HER2 so the therapy had less to target.
4. It ran out of moneyCosts rose after its 2023 reverse merger with Sesen Bio. Cash fell from $77.6M (end of 2023) to $2.8M (Sep 2025) through three rounds of layoffs. A planned merger collapsed in Sep 2025 when the partner couldn't raise its financing, and Nasdaq delisted the stock in Oct 2025.

Where Thunder differs: Thunder's lead program reprograms macrophages inside the body (in vivo), so there is no per-patient manufacturing. Its lead target, mesothelin, is found across many cancer types rather than in a crowded HER2 market. Carisma itself was pivoting to in vivo delivery when its money ran out, and it shelved a mesothelin program for lack of financing, not because of data. Its in vivo technology now sits with Moderna, which holds a fully paid-up license. Carisma vs. Thunder →

What the deals show

Buyers pay before human data

Lilly paid up to $2.4B for Orna and Bristol Myers Squibb paid about $1.5B for Orbital while both were preclinical, and J&J put $785M into preclinical Sail. Platform technology alone has commanded large prices.

Prices are climbing fast

In vivo CAR deal sizes rose from $350M (Interius, Aug 2025) to as much as $7B (Kelonia, Apr 2026) in eight months, as more large companies competed for a limited number of platforms.

Macrophages are the next frontier

Every in vivo company bought so far targets T cells. No macrophage-engineering company has been acquired, and the only in vivo CAR-macrophage human data so far comes from CREATE Medicines, which remains independent.

Autoimmune is a major draw

Four of the eight in vivo deals (Capstan, Orbital, Orna and Sail) are aimed at autoimmune disease, not cancer. Thunder's M2 program, which calms inflammation, points at the same market.

Mesothelin has been bought before

Kite's purchase of Tmunity included a Phase 1 mesothelin-targeted CAR-T for solid tumors, the same target as Thunder's lead MOTO-CAR program.

What Sets Thunder Apart

Thunder is an early-stage company in a field where the first movers stumbled and the money is moving to in vivo approaches. Here is where it stands out.

Early, foundational patents

Thunder's macrophage-CAR filings date back to 2015, years before any CAR-macrophage therapy entered human testing. The portfolio now spans 28 filings in 10 jurisdictions, with 18 issued (9 in the US) and 10 still pending.

Built for in vivo

Thunder's lead program uses lipid nanoparticles, the same delivery approach behind the field's first in vivo human data and behind several of the large pharma acquisitions above.

Both directions of the switch

Thunder's patents describe pushing macrophages toward M1 to fight cancer and toward M2 to calm inflammation. That supports two markets: solid tumors and inflammatory/autoimmune disease.

Learned from the first wave

Carisma, the leading ex vivo company, dropped its HER2 programs amid crowded HER2 competition and tumors losing the HER2 marker. Thunder's in vivo approach avoids cell manufacturing, and its lead target, mesothelin, is expressed across many cancer types. Carisma vs. Thunder →

Deep scientific founder

Founder and CSO Kim O'Neill, PhD, has 40 years of cancer research experience at Brigham Young University, 155 peer-reviewed papers and 9 issued patents.

Off-the-shelf and short-lived

MOTO-CAR is designed as an IV-injected, off-the-shelf treatment with temporary CAR expression: no cell manufacturing, no specialized facilities, and a built-in limit on how long the engineered receptor stays active.

What the patents describe

The receptor

A chimeric receptor built from an outer targeting domain, a transmembrane domain, and an inner signaling domain taken from receptors that polarize macrophages when activated.

The switch

Binding the target activates the receptor and drives the macrophage toward M1 (anti-tumor) or M2 (anti-inflammatory), depending on design.

Broad building blocks

Signaling domains described include TLR3, TLR4, TLR7, TLR8, TLR9, MYD88, IRAK1, FCGR2A, FCGR3A and FCER1G. Targets described include mesothelin, HER2, GPC3, CEA, ROR1, EGFRvIII, GD2, BCMA, CD19 and others.

Investor FAQ

Short answers to the questions investors ask most. Each links back to the detail above.

The science
What does Thunder Biotech do?

Thunder is developing MOTO-CAR, an immunotherapy that reprograms macrophages, a type of immune cell, to attack solid tumors. Its lead program delivers the instructions directly inside the body using lipid nanoparticles, the same delivery technology used in mRNA vaccines.

What is a CAR-macrophage, and how is it different from CAR-T?

Both add a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) to an immune cell so it can recognize a cancer marker. CAR-T uses T cells and has transformed blood cancers, but it has struggled in solid tumors, which make up most cancer cases.

Macrophages already gather in solid tumors in large numbers, can engulf tumor cells directly, and recruit T cells to join the attack. They also lack a T-cell receptor, so they cannot cause graft-versus-host disease, and reviews report lower cytokine-release risk than CAR-T. More in The Science

How does MOTO-CAR work?

A lipid nanoparticle carries genetic instructions for the CAR to macrophages. When the CAR finds its target on a cancer cell, it flips the macrophage from a tumor-protecting "M2" state into a cancer-fighting "M1" state. The activated macrophage attacks the tumor and brings in T cells, which may build immune memory against recurrence. See the four steps

How is MOTO-CAR given, and does it stay in the body?

It is designed to be given by IV injection as an off-the-shelf product, with no need to collect and engineer a patient's cells. The nanoparticles travel to the tumor and are taken up by tumor macrophages. MOTO-CARs are short-lived: they are expressed temporarily, which is intended to limit long-term side effects, while the immune response they trigger, including T-cell memory, can last. See the mechanism diagram

Why target mesothelin?

Mesothelin is a marker found on many cancer types, which gives one program a broad potential reach. It also avoids the crowded HER2 space, which contributed to the first-generation CAR-macrophage company dropping its programs. Thunder's patents describe many other targets as well, including HER2, GPC3, CEA and ROR1. [Rob to confirm: lead indication(s), e.g. mesothelioma, ovarian, pancreatic]

What does "in vivo" mean, and why does it matter?

In vivo means the cells are programmed inside the patient's body. The first wave of CAR-macrophage therapies removed cells, engineered them in a lab and infused them back, which is slow and expensive. Programming cells in the body skips manufacturing altogether. This is where the field and large pharma investment have moved, and it is the approach in Thunder's lead program.

Risk and opportunity
The first CAR-macrophage company shut down. Why should investors still believe in the field?

Carisma Therapeutics, which used the ex vivo approach, wound down in 2025 after dropping its HER2 programs. Its challenges were largely tied to that model: costly cell manufacturing, crowded HER2 competition, and tumors losing the HER2 marker.

The in vivo approach avoids manufacturing entirely, and it is producing the field's first encouraging human signals: in November 2025, CREATE Medicines reported a confirmed partial response and engineered cells inside tumors in a 27-patient Phase 1 study. Those are early results from a small study, not proof of efficacy. Carisma vs. Thunder →

Is large pharma interested in this kind of technology?

Yes. Large pharma acquired twelve companies developing engineered immune-cell therapies between August 2021 and April 2026, with about $27 billion in combined headline value. The fastest-moving group is in vivo CAR: AstraZeneca, AbbVie, Gilead/Kite, Bristol Myers Squibb and Eli Lilly (twice) bought six in vivo companies in 14 months, in deals worth $350 million to $7 billion, including at least one at the preclinical stage. Those companies focus on T cells rather than macrophages, but they show the value placed on in vivo platforms. See Deals & Valuations

What are the main risks?

Like any preclinical biotech, results in animals may not carry over to people, and clinical development takes years and significant capital. Specific to the field: engineered macrophages may not persist long enough in the body, nanoparticles tend to accumulate in the liver and spleen, and the degree of cell reprogramming is hard to standardize. Thunder also competes with larger, better-funded companies.

Appendix

Carisma Therapeutics: what happened, and why Thunder is different

Carisma was the first company to put a CAR-macrophage into patients, and its 2025 wind-down is the most common question investors ask about this field. Here is what happened and how Thunder's approach differs on each point.

The short answer

Carisma's therapy was safe, and it did what the science predicted: the engineered macrophages reached tumors, reshaped the tumor environment and expanded cancer-fighting T cells. Carisma did not fail because macrophages don't work. It failed because of how it made its therapy, what it aimed it at, and how fast it spent its money.

Why the same story is unlikely to repeat at Thunder

No manufacturing bottleneck

Carisma's biggest structural cost, making cells for every patient, does not exist in Thunder's in vivo model.

A better target

Mesothelin avoids the crowded HER2 market and the antigen-loss problem Carisma cited when it dropped CT-0525.

Already where the field moved

Carisma was pivoting to in vivo delivery when it ran out of money. Thunder started there, and in vivo is where large pharma is now buying.

Validation, not a warning

Carisma's trial showed CAR-macrophages are safe in people and reach tumors. That de-risks the cell type Thunder uses.

What Thunder still has to manage. Thunder is preclinical and faces the risks every early biotech does: results in mice may not carry over to people, and reaching the clinic takes years and significant capital. The lesson from Carisma is to keep spending disciplined and raise money ahead of need, which is part of why Thunder's approach avoids costly per-patient manufacturing.
